🔐 Cyberattacks & Breaches

Barclays’ US banking arm confirms data breach

Barclays Bank Delaware confirmed that an unauthorized third party accessed its credit card application status system, exposing affected customers’ names, contact details, dates of birth, and Social Security numbers. The bank said the breach was limited to credit card applications, reported it to law enforcement and regulators, and is offering 24 months of identity monitoring and protection services.

Hackers breach US Air Force flagship Cloud One computing platform

Hackers breached the US Air Force’s Cloud One platform, which hosts personnel, logistics, and tactical applications. The discovery paused some migrations, blocked application updates, and revoked administrator rights for some users. Cyber Command was called in to assess the damage, while the hackers’ identity, full scope, and whether data was stolen remain unconfirmed.

  • Hackers breach US Air Force flagship Cloud One computing platform — cybernews.com

McDonald’s Amazon servers attacked, hackers say they stole customer data

A hacker group called Inception claims it stole 293GB of data from McDonald’s AWS storage and internal tools, including 938,667 unique email addresses—about 370,000 tied to employees—and customer data such as names, phone numbers, loyalty IDs, birth dates, and partial payment card details. McDonald’s has not confirmed the breach, and the group threatens to release the files unless contacted within 24 hours.

  • McDonald’s Amazon servers attacked, hackers say they stole customer data — cybernews.com

Hackers leak data stolen from internet landlord GoDaddy

Ransomware gang Inception has posted about 37.5GB of data allegedly stolen from GoDaddy, including roughly 25GB of meeting and all-hands videos, documents, code files, configurations, and employee PII. GoDaddy has not publicly acknowledged the incident, and Inception listed no ransom amount or breach date. Researchers say the files’ 2020–2024 timestamps may limit impact, but exposed employee data could support targeted scam campaigns.

The Cutthroat Business of Shipwrecks

In 1627, two Portuguese “floating cities” shipwrecked off Bordeaux, spilling luxury goods onto French beaches and sparking a dispute between cargo owners and coastal residents who claimed the goods under the ancient “right of wreck.” The custom gave local lords or finders claims to wrecked property, but centuries of legal wrangling reshaped it as international trade grew. Courts developed distinctions such as flotsam and jetsam, and owners were generally entitled to recover their goods in exchange for a fee or salvage reward.
